A/B testing

An A/B test shows the old version (A) to some users and the new version (B) to others, picked at random, and compares what they do. It's how apps like Swiggy, Flipkart and Instagram decide whether a change is really better.

1. Design an A/B test for a new checkout button. What do you measure, and for how long?

Random split by user → one main number → a few "must not get worse" numbers → run full weeks → decide once at the end.

Say it in the interview
  1. Goal: more people finish buying.
  2. Split: randomly put each user in A or B, and keep them there every visit.
  3. Who counts: only people who reach the checkout page, because only they see the button.
  4. Main number: out of people who reach checkout, how many buy.
  5. Must not get worse: money spent per user, returns or cancellations, page speed.
  6. How long: long enough to get enough users (next question), and in full weeks, because people shop differently on weekends.
  7. At the end: first check the split really was 50/50, then check whether B's result is a real difference or luck.
Everyday exampleA cricket toss decides who bats first, so neither captain can pick the easier conditions. Random splitting does the same: it makes the A and B groups alike, so any difference comes from the button and not from who happened to be in each group.

Key words: hypothesis, randomise by user, primary metric, guardrail metrics, run full weeks.

2. How do you work out the sample size for a test?

Small change → many users. Want to spot half the change? You need about four times the users.

Say it in the interviewI need to know four things: how the metric does today (say 10% of visitors buy), the smallest improvement worth catching (say 10% to 11%), how sure I want to be that a "win" isn't luck (usually 95%), and how likely I want to be to catch a real improvement (usually 80%). I put these into an online sample-size calculator or Python. The thing to remember: smaller improvements need many more users.
Everyday exampleIs a coin a little unfair (51% heads)? You'd need thousands of flips to tell. A badly unfair coin (80% heads) shows itself in about 20 flips. Small differences need lots of data.

Key words: baseline rate, minimum detectable effect (MDE), significance level 5%, power 80%.

3. What is statistical power, and how do you increase it?

Power = the chance your test catches a real improvement. Aim for 80%.

Say it in the interviewPower is how likely the test is to notice an effect that really exists. With low power, a good change can look like "no difference" and get thrown away. To raise power: get more users or run longer, test a bigger change, or use a less noisy metric (for example, clicks on the button rather than total revenue, which jumps around a lot).

More users make both curves thinner, so they overlap less: fewer false wins, fewer missed wins, more power.

Everyday exampleLooking for a dropped earring in a dark room. Power is your chance of finding it if it's really there. A brighter torch (more users) or a bigger earring (a bigger change) makes it easier to find.

Key words: power = 1 − β, false negative (Type II error), 80% power, variance reduction (CUPED).

4. Your test ends with p = 0.06. What do you tell the product manager?

Not proven, but not proven useless either. Don't stretch the rules to make it pass.

Say it in the interviewWe agreed a result counts only if p is below 0.05, so this isn't a proven win. But it isn't proof that B is useless either: the likely effect ranges from about zero to a small improvement. Then it's a business call: if the change is cheap and easy to undo, we might ship it knowing the evidence is weak; otherwise we run a bigger test. What I won't do is run the test longer or dig through user groups until something looks significant.
Everyday exampleThe pass mark is 40 and you scored 38. The teacher doesn't round it up because you were close, but 38 also doesn't mean you know nothing. Re-taking the exam properly is fair; changing the pass mark after seeing the score isn't.

Key words: pre-agreed significance level, confidence interval, practical vs statistical significance, p-hacking.

5. Why is checking the results every day a problem?

Fix the end date before you start. Look if you like, but don't stop early because it looks good.

Say it in the interviewEarly in a test, results swing up and down by luck. If you check every day and stop the first time it looks significant, you'll often catch one of those lucky swings and declare a false win. So decide the test length before starting and judge it once at the end. If the team really needs early looks, use a method designed for that (sequential testing). Watching daily for bugs is fine.
Everyday exampleDeclaring the winner of a T20 match after the 6th over because one team is ahead. Teams are often ahead early by luck; the result only counts after 20 overs.

Key words: peeking, inflated false positives, fixed sample size, sequential testing.

6. You tracked 10 metrics and one is significant. Is it a win?

Check enough numbers and one will look good by luck. Pick one main metric before the test.

Say it in the interviewProbably not. If you check 10 metrics, there's roughly a 40% chance that at least one looks significant just by luck. That's why we choose one main metric before the test starts. If several metrics really matter, we use a stricter bar for each one (for example, divide 0.05 by the number of metrics, called the Bonferroni correction). A surprise result on a side metric is an idea for the next test, not a reason to launch.
Everyday exampleBuy one lottery ticket and you'll almost surely lose. Buy ten and your chance of a small win goes up, without you getting any luckier. Each extra metric is another ticket.

Key words: multiple comparisons, primary metric, Bonferroni correction, false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg).

7. The treatment group got 52% of users instead of 50%. What does that tell you?

Uneven split in a big test = something is broken. Fix it before trusting any result.

Say it in the interviewThat's called a sample ratio mismatch. With a few hundred users, 52/48 can be luck. With 100,000 users it almost never is, and a quick chi-square test would confirm that. It usually means a bug: for example, the new version crashes or loads slowly, so some B users leave before they're counted. I wouldn't trust any result from the test until we found and fixed the cause.
Everyday exampleA shop hands out two kinds of free samples, half and half. At closing, one counter has given out far fewer. With 10 customers that's luck; with 10,000 it means something went wrong at that counter, like a long queue that made people give up.

Key words: sample ratio mismatch (SRM), chi-square test, sanity checks.

8. Conversion went up but revenue per user went down. Do you ship?

More buyers but smaller bills. The answer depends on the goal you agreed before the test.

Say it in the interviewMore people bought, but each spent less, so total money per user fell. Before deciding, I'd check: is the revenue drop real or noise? Are people switching to cheaper products? Is it just early excitement about something new? Do the new buyers come back later? If the goal was revenue, I wouldn't ship. If the goal was winning new customers and they're worth more over time, I might. Ideally the team agrees on the main goal before the test, so this situation has a ready answer.

Everyday exampleA restaurant puts a cheap thali on the menu. More people come in, but most order the thali instead of a full meal, so the restaurant earns less per customer.

Key words: guardrail metric, average order value, mix shift, novelty effect, long-term value.

9. A feature can't be A/B tested, for example a city-wide launch. How do you measure its impact?

Compare the change in the launch city with the change in a similar city that didn't get the feature.

Say it in the interviewI'd use difference-in-differences. Orders in the launch city went up by 300 a day; in a similar city without the launch they went up by 100, from things like festivals or weather that affect both. So the feature's effect is about 200. This only works if the two cities were moving together before the launch, so I'd check that first. Other options are building a "fake" comparison city from several similar cities (synthetic control), or comparing with what the past trend predicted.
Everyday exampleYou start eating healthy and lose 3 kg in a month. Your roommate, who changed nothing, lost 1 kg in the same hot exam month. So the diet's real effect is about 2 kg.

Key words: quasi-experiment, difference-in-differences, parallel trends, synthetic control.

10. In a social or marketplace app, users in the test affect each other. Why does that break a normal A/B test, and what do you do?

If A and B users share drivers, sellers or friends, test whole groups at once instead of single users.

Say it in the interviewA normal test assumes one user's version doesn't affect anyone else. In a ride app, if B riders get a discount, they book more and take drivers away from A riders, so A looks worse than it really is and B's win looks bigger than it is. The fix is to give the same version to whole groups that don't mix much: whole cities, or whole friend groups in a social app. Another way is to switch an entire city between A and B by time, for example every hour (a switchback test). The cost is fewer independent groups, so the test needs to run longer.
Everyday exampleHalf a hostel gets free Maggi coupons from the mess. Those students rush to the mess, the queue grows, and the students without coupons wait longer and eat less. Now the coupon group looks extra happy compared with an unfairly unhappy other group.

Key words: network effects, interference, cluster (geo) randomisation, switchback test.

Practical ML mistakes

1. Your fraud model has 99% accuracy and 1% of transactions are fraud. Is it good?

When the thing you're hunting is rare, accuracy lies. Ask how many frauds it catches and how many of its alarms are right.

Say it in the interviewNot necessarily. A model that always says "not fraud" is also 99% accurate and catches zero fraud. I'd look at recall (of all the fraud, how much did we catch?) and precision (of the transactions we flagged, how many were really fraud?). To help the model learn the rare class, I'd tell it that fraud mistakes count more (class weights), or balance the training data, and then choose the alarm cut-off carefully.

Everyday exampleIn Jaipur, a weather app that always says "no rain" is right on most days, and useless on the one day you needed an umbrella. Recall asks: of the rainy days, how many did it warn you about? Precision asks: when it said rain, how often did it rain?

Key words: class imbalance, precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC, class weights, resampling (only on training data).

2. What is data leakage? Give two examples and how you prevent it.

Leakage = the model peeks at answers it won't have in real life. Great practice score, poor real score.

Say it in the interviewLeakage is when training data contains information the model won't have at prediction time. Example one: predicting loan default using "number of recovery calls made"; those calls only happen after someone defaults. Example two: filling missing values with the average of the whole dataset before splitting, so the test data quietly influences training. To prevent it: split the data first, do all cleaning steps on the training part only (a scikit-learn Pipeline does this for you), and for each feature ask, "would I really know this at the moment of prediction?"
Everyday exampleA student who saw the question paper before the mock exam scores 100, then fails the real exam. The marks were real, but they didn't measure what they were supposed to.

Key words: target leakage, train-test contamination, Pipeline, "available at prediction time".

3. How do you split the data for a time-series or per-user problem?

Train on the past, test on the future. Keep each person entirely on one side.

Say it in the interviewFor data over time, I never split randomly, because then the model learns from the future. I train on earlier months and test on later ones, repeating this a few times as the window moves forward (walk-forward validation). When there are several rows per user or patient, I keep all of one person's rows together, all in training or all in testing; otherwise the model just recognises people it has already seen.
Everyday exampleYou can't practise predicting this IPL season using results from next season. And a teacher who tests students with the exact questions they practised learns nothing about whether they understood.

Key words: time-based split, walk-forward validation (TimeSeriesSplit), group split (GroupKFold).

4. How do you choose the classification threshold?

Set the alarm level by asking which mistake costs more.

Say it in the interviewThe model gives a score from 0 to 1, and 0.5 is only a default cut-off. I'd pick it based on what each mistake costs. Missing a fraud might cost ₹5,000, while wrongly blocking a good customer costs much less, so for fraud I'd use a lower cut-off and catch more, accepting some extra false alarms. I'd try different cut-offs on validation data and pick the one with the lowest total cost, or the one that meets a target like "catch 90% of fraud".
Everyday exampleAn airport metal detector set too sensitive beeps at every belt buckle and the queue never moves; set too loose, it misses real danger. Airports choose sensitive, because missing danger costs far more than an extra check. A spam filter does the opposite: losing an important email is worse than seeing one spam message.

Key words: decision threshold, precision–recall trade-off, cost of false positives vs false negatives, calibration.

5. Your model was great offline but is worse in production. Why?

The real world isn't the practice data: inputs changed, behaviour changed, or the practice test was flawed.

Say it in the interviewFour usual reasons. One: the live system builds features differently from my notebook (for example, missing values filled differently). Two: the incoming data has changed, like new types of users or a festival season. Three: behaviour itself changed; fraudsters learn new tricks. Four: the offline score was too good because of leakage. I'd compare live inputs with the training data, check results by user group and over time, fix the pipeline, and retrain on recent data. Then I'd set up monitoring so this gets caught early next time.
Everyday exampleA batter who practised only on flat home nets struggles on a bouncy overseas pitch. The skill was real, but the conditions changed.

Key words: training-serving skew, data drift, concept drift, monitoring, retraining.

6. How do you handle missing values and outliers?

First ask why. Fill gaps with the middle value plus a "was missing" flag. Fix typos, cap real extremes.

Say it in the interviewFor missing values, I first ask why they're missing, because sometimes the blank itself tells you something. Then: drop a column if it's mostly empty; fill numbers with the median and categories with "Unknown"; and add a yes/no column saying the value was missing. For outliers, I check whether they're mistakes, like age 250, and fix or remove those. If they're real but extreme, I cap them or use a model that isn't thrown off by them, such as tree-based models. In fraud detection, the outliers may be exactly what we're looking for.
Everyday exampleTen friends in a room earn about ₹30,000 a month each. Mukesh Ambani walks in, and the average income becomes crores, while the median (the middle person) barely moves. That's why we fill gaps with the median. Blanks can mean something too: if people skip "income" on a form, the skippers are often the high earners.

Key words: median imputation, missing indicator, IQR rule, capping (winsorising), robust models.

7. How would you explain feature importance to a non-technical stakeholder, and what are its limits?

"Mix up one input and see how much worse the model gets." It shows what the model uses, not what causes the result.

Say it in the interviewFor the whole model: "if we scramble the 'days since last login' column, the model gets much worse, so it relies on that most." That's permutation importance. For one customer, SHAP values split their score into parts: "this customer's risk is high mainly because they haven't logged in for 30 days." The limits: it shows what the model uses, not what causes people to leave; two similar features can share or swap importance; and a feature that seems far too important can be a sign of leakage.
Everyday exampleTo see who matters most in a cricket team, drop one player at a time and watch how much the team's results fall. And remember: ice-cream sales and swimming accidents both rise in summer. They move together, but ice cream doesn't cause accidents.

Key words: permutation importance, SHAP values, correlation is not causation.

Product questions

"How would you measure the success of Instagram Reels?"

Goal → users → one main number → a few supporting numbers → what must not get worse.

Say it in the interview
  1. Goal: keep people watching short videos on Instagram and keep creators posting (instead of moving to YouTube Shorts).
  2. Users: viewers (watch, like, share) and creators (post).
  3. Main number: how many daily users watch at least one reel.
  4. Supporting: reels shared per viewer, creators posting every week, and whether reel watchers keep coming back.
  5. Must not get worse: time on the feed and stories (are reels just stealing from them?), total app time, and reports or "not interested" taps.
Everyday exampleA new dish at a restaurant isn't a success just because people order it. You also check they come back for it, and that it isn't simply replacing the dishes that earn more.

Practise the same steps on: YouTube Shorts, Swiggy Instamart, LinkedIn Easy Apply, a new Spotify playlist feature.

"Swiggy orders fell 8% last week. How do you investigate?"

Is the data right? → What changed? → Where is the drop? → Which step lost people?

Say it in the interview
  1. Clarify: orders placed or delivered? Compared with last week, or the same week last year? Sudden or slow?
  2. Is the data right? Maybe the tracking or dashboard broke. Do payment records show the same drop?
  3. What changed? An app update, a price or delivery-fee change, an outage. Outside: a festival, an India match, heavy rain, a Zomato offer.
  4. Where is the drop? Android or iPhone, which app version, which cities, new or old users, lunch or dinner.
  5. Which step lost people? Follow the journey from opening the app to paying (picture below).
  6. Conclude: "Payments started failing on the new Android version after Tuesday's update, so roll back that change and check that orders recover."
Everyday exampleIf your phone suddenly stops charging, you check in order: is the socket on, is it the cable, is it the adapter, is it the phone? Same idea: check each step until you find the broken one.

Key words: data sanity check, segmentation, funnel analysis, root cause.
