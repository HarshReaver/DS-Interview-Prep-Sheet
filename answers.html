A/B testing

1. Design an A/B test for a new checkout button. What do you measure, and for how long?

Say it in an interviewGoal: more completed purchases. I'd randomise by user, only count users who reach checkout, and use checkout conversion as the one primary metric, with revenue per user, refunds and page load time as guardrails. I'd size the test from the baseline conversion, the smallest lift worth shipping, 5% significance and 80% power, run it for whole weeks, check the split is really 50/50, and decide once at the end.

The whole idea

  1. Hypothesis. "The new button increases the share of checkout visitors who complete a purchase."
  2. Unit of randomisation: the user, not the page view or session. Otherwise one person sees both buttons, and their visits aren't independent. Hashing the user id keeps each user in the same group every visit.
  3. Who to count: only users who reach the checkout page. Counting everyone dilutes the effect with people who never saw the button and makes the test need far more users.
  4. Metrics.
    • Primary (decides the launch): checkout conversion = purchases ÷ users who reached checkout.
    • Guardrails (must not get worse): revenue per user, average order value, refunds or cancellations, page load time, error rate.
    • Sanity (invariant) metrics: number of users in each group, which should match the planned split.
  5. Size and duration: work out users per group from the sample-size formula (prompt 2), then divide by daily checkout traffic. Round up to whole weeks, because weekday and weekend shoppers behave differently, and run at least 1–2 weeks to let the novelty effect fade.
  6. Launch safely: start at 1–5% of traffic for a day to catch bugs, then go to 50/50.
  7. Analyse once at the planned end: sanity checks first, then a two-proportion z-test with a confidence interval, then compare the lift with the smallest lift worth shipping.

Likely follow-ups

  • "What if the test shows a big lift on day 2?" Don't stop: see prompt 5.
  • "What if conversion rises but revenue falls?" See prompt 8.

2. How do you work out the sample size for a test?

Say it in an interviewI need four things: the baseline rate, the minimum detectable effect (the smallest change worth detecting), the significance level (usually 5%) and the power (usually 80%). For a conversion metric, a good rule of thumb is n per group ≈ 16·p(1−p)/δ². The key intuition: halving the effect you want to detect needs four times as many users.

The formula

For two groups and a conversion rate p, with an absolute difference δ to detect:

n per group = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² × 2·p(1−p) / δ²
            = (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2·p(1−p) / δ²   ≈ 16 · p(1−p) / δ²   (α = 0.05, power = 80%)

Worked example

Baseline conversion 10%, and the business only cares about a lift to 11% (δ = 1 percentage point):

n ≈ 16 × 0.10 × 0.90 / 0.01² = 16 × 0.09 / 0.0001 = 14,400 users per group  →  28,800 in total

With 2,000 checkout users a day, that's 14.4 days, so you'd run 3 full weeks. Want to detect 0.5 pp instead? 57,600 per group, four times as many.

Users needed fall with the square of the effect size.

Likely follow-ups

  • "Where does the minimum detectable effect come from?" From the business: the smallest lift that pays for building and maintaining the change.
  • "Not enough traffic?" Test a bigger change, use a metric that's closer to the change (button clicks rather than revenue), reduce variance (CUPED, see prompt 3), or accept a longer test.

3. What is statistical power, and how do you increase it?

Say it in an interviewPower is the chance the test detects an effect when one really exists, so 1 minus the false-negative rate β. We usually aim for 80%. You raise it with more users, a bigger effect, or a less noisy metric, for example with CUPED, which uses each user's pre-experiment behaviour to remove noise. A higher significance level also raises power, but buys it with more false positives.

Left curve: the results you'd see if B does nothing; right curve: if B truly helps. Red = false positive rate α, orange = false negative rate β. More users make both curves narrower, so they overlap less and power goes up.

The whole idea

  • α (5%): chance of calling a win when there's no effect. β (20%): chance of missing a real effect. Power = 1 − β = 80%.
  • Ways to raise power: more users or a longer test; a bigger change; a metric closer to the change (less noise); variance reduction: CUPED adjusts each user's metric with their pre-experiment value, capping extreme values (winsorising) also works; a one-sided test only if you decided that before the test.
  • Why low power hurts twice: you miss real effects, and the "wins" you do find tend to overstate the effect (the winner's curse).

4. Your test ends with p = 0.06. What do you tell the product manager?

Say it in an interviewAt our agreed 5% bar, this isn't significant, so we can't claim a win. I'd show the confidence interval, for example +0.8% with a range from about 0% to +1.6%: the true effect could be nothing or could be worth having. Then the decision is a business one: if the change is cheap and low-risk we might ship it knowing the evidence is weak, or we rerun a properly powered test. What I wouldn't do is extend the test or slice segments until something turns significant.

The whole idea

  • What p = 0.06 means: if B truly made no difference, results at least this extreme would show up 6% of the time. It does not mean "94% chance B is better".
  • Significance isn't importance: report the effect size and its confidence interval, and compare it with the smallest effect that matters to the business.
  • Options: (1) don't ship; (2) ship on business grounds if it's cheap and reversible, stating the evidence honestly; (3) run a new test sized for the effect you care about.
  • Mistakes to avoid (p-hacking): extending the test until p < 0.05, checking segments until one is significant, switching to a one-sided test after seeing the data.

5. Why is checking the results every day a problem?

Say it in an interviewThe p-value moves around a lot early in a test. If you stop the first time it dips below 0.05, every look is another chance to catch a false positive, so the real false-positive rate can climb from 5% to 20% or more. Fix the sample size in advance and decide once, or use a sequential test designed for repeated looks. Watching the dashboard for bugs is fine; stopping early because it looks like a win isn't.

An A/A-like test with no real effect: the p-value wanders, and a daily peeker would have stopped on day 6 with a false win.

The numbers

Equally spaced looks, each at p < 0.05151020
Real chance of a false positive5%~14%~19%~25%

Fixes

  • Decide the sample size up front (prompt 2) and analyse once.
  • Sequential testing with alpha spending (for example O'Brien–Fleming): very strict thresholds at early looks, close to 0.05 at the end.
  • Always-valid p-values (used by some experiment platforms) are built for continuous monitoring.

6. You tracked 10 metrics and one is significant. Is it a win?

Say it in an interviewProbably not on its own. With 10 independent metrics at 5%, the chance that at least one comes out significant by luck is 1 − 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 40%. That's why you declare one primary metric before the test. If several metrics matter, correct for multiple comparisons: Bonferroni (divide 0.05 by the number of tests) is strict; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the share of false discoveries and keeps more power. A surprise result on a secondary metric is a hypothesis for the next test, not a launch decision.

Worked example: 5 metrics, p-values sorted

Rank kp-valueBonferroni: p < 0.05/5 = 0.01?BH: p ≤ (k/5)·0.05?
10.003yes0.010 → yes
20.012no0.020 → yes
30.021no0.030 → yes
40.040no0.040 → yes
50.300no0.050 → no

Benjamini–Hochberg: find the largest k where p(k) ≤ (k/m)·q, and call ranks 1..k significant. The same problem shows up with many variants (A/B/C/D) and with slicing results by many segments.

7. The treatment group got 52% of users instead of 50%. What does that tell you?

Say it in an interviewThat's a sample ratio mismatch, and with a large sample it's almost never chance. I'd run a chi-square test on the counts; if it fails, something in assignment or logging is broken, so I wouldn't trust any metric from the test until I'd found the cause and rerun it.

Check it with a chi-square test

100,000 users, expected 50,000 / 50,000, observed 52,000 / 48,000
χ² = (52,000 − 50,000)²/50,000 + (48,000 − 50,000)²/50,000 = 80 + 80 = 160   →  p ≈ 10⁻³⁶

Compare 520 vs 480 out of 1,000: χ² = 1.6, p ≈ 0.2, which is normal noise. The same percentage means very different things at different sample sizes.

Common causes

  • The new version is slower or crashes, so some B users leave before they're logged.
  • Bot filtering or redirects affect one group more.
  • A bug in the hashing or assignment code.
  • Whether a user counts in the test depends on the variant they saw.

8. Conversion went up but revenue per user went down. Do you ship?

Say it in an interviewIt depends on the goal we agreed before the test. Revenue per user = conversion × average order value, so more people are buying, but they're spending less each. I'd check whether the revenue drop is significant, which products shifted, whether it's a novelty effect, and whether the new buyers come back. If revenue or profit is the goal and it fell, I wouldn't ship; if the goal is getting new customers and their long-term value makes up for it, I might.

Worked example

ConversionAverage orderRevenue per user
A (control)5.0%₹1,000₹50.00
B (new)5.5%₹850₹46.75

B made buying easier but pushed people toward cheaper items (a mix shift). Things to check: segments (new vs returning users), effect over time (novelty), margin rather than revenue, and repeat purchases over the next weeks.

The lesson for interviews: agree on one overall evaluation criterion and its guardrails before the test, so a result like this has a pre-agreed answer.

9. A feature can't be A/B tested, for example a city-wide launch. How do you measure its impact?

Say it in an interviewI'd use a quasi-experiment. The simplest is difference-in-differences: compare the change in the launch city with the change in a similar city that didn't get the feature over the same period. That removes trends both cities share, like festivals or seasons. The key assumption is parallel trends, which I'd check on the weeks before launch. Alternatives are synthetic control (a weighted mix of several cities that matches the launch city before launch) and interrupted time series.
effect = (1,300 − 1,000) − (900 − 800) = 300 − 100 = +200 orders a day

Other options

  • Synthetic control: build a "fake" control city as a weighted average of other cities that tracks the launch city closely before launch.
  • Interrupted time series: forecast what would have happened from the pre-launch trend, and compare with what did happen.
  • Matching or propensity scores: for opt-in features, compare users who opted in with similar users who didn't.

10. In a social or marketplace app, users in the test affect each other. Why does that break a normal A/B test, and what do you do?

Say it in an interviewA normal A/B test assumes one user's group doesn't affect another user's outcome. In a ride app, if treatment riders get a discount, they take drivers away from control riders, so control looks worse and the effect is overstated. In a social app, a new sharing feature reaches control users through their friends. The fix is to randomise bigger units that don't interact much: whole cities or friend clusters, or switch the whole market between A and B over time (switchback tests). You pay for it with fewer units and so less power.

Options

  • Cluster (geo) randomisation: whole cities or regions get one version.
  • Graph-cluster randomisation: for social apps, groups of friends that mostly interact with each other.
  • Switchback tests: the whole market alternates between A and B in time slots (for example every hour); common in ride and delivery apps.

Practical ML pitfalls

1. Your fraud model has 99% accuracy and 1% of transactions are fraud. Is it good?

Say it in an interviewNot necessarily. A model that always says "not fraud" also gets 99% accuracy and catches nothing. With rare positives I'd look at precision (of the flagged transactions, how many are fraud), recall (of the fraud, how much we catch) and PR-AUC. To handle the imbalance I'd use class weights or resample only the training folds, keep stratified validation, and tune the decision threshold to the cost of each mistake.

The always-"not fraud" model on 10,000 transactions

Predicted fraudPredicted not fraud
Actually fraud (100)TP = 0FN = 100
Actually not fraud (9,900)FP = 0TN = 9,900

Accuracy = 9,900 / 10,000 = 99%. Recall = 0 / 100 = 0%. Useless.

The whole idea

  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP); recall = TP / (TP + FN); F1 is their harmonic mean.
  • PR-AUC is more informative than ROC-AUC when positives are rare, because ROC's false-positive rate stays tiny even when you flag lots of good transactions.
  • Fixes: class weights (class_weight='balanced'), undersampling or SMOTE applied only to training folds (never before the split), stratified K-fold, threshold tuning (pitfall 4), and more fraud labels if possible.

2. What is data leakage? Give two examples and how you prevent it.

Say it in an interviewLeakage is when training uses information that won't be available at prediction time, so offline scores look great and the model fails in production. Example one: a "chargeback filed" feature in a fraud model; it's only known after the fraud. Example two: fitting the scaler or imputer on the whole dataset before splitting, so test-set statistics leak into training. I prevent it by splitting first, putting all preprocessing inside a pipeline that's fitted per fold, splitting by time or by user when needed, and checking for every feature: would I know this at the moment of prediction?

Common kinds of leakage

  • Target leakage: a feature created from or after the outcome (chargeback flag, "account closed" for a churn model).
  • Preprocessing leakage: scaling, imputing, feature selection or oversampling done before the split.
  • Duplicate or group leakage: the same customer or patient in both train and test.
  • Time leakage: a random split on time-ordered data, or rolling features that include the current day.

Warning signs: scores that look too good, or one feature with overwhelming importance.

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
model = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())
cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5)   # the imputer and scaler are refitted inside every fold

3. How do you split the data for a time-series or per-user problem?

Say it in an interviewFor time series I'd never split randomly, because that lets the model learn from the future. I'd train on the past and test on the period after it, and use walk-forward validation: several folds where the training window grows and the test window moves forward. For per-user data I'd use a grouped split, so all of one user's rows are in train or in test, never both; otherwise the model memorises users and the score is too optimistic.
  • scikit-learn: TimeSeriesSplit for time, GroupKFold (or StratifiedGroupKFold) for users or patients.
  • If features use windows (for example "orders in the last 7 days"), leave a gap between train and test so the windows don't overlap.
  • The final test set should be the most recent period, because that's closest to what the model will face.

4. How do you choose the classification threshold?

Say it in an interview0.5 is just a default. I'd choose the threshold from the cost of each mistake: if a missed fraud costs ₹5,000 and a wrongly blocked customer costs ₹100, I'd compute the total cost on the validation set at each threshold and pick the cheapest. Or, if the business sets a target like "catch 90% of fraud", I'd take the threshold with the best precision that still meets it. If the scores will be read as probabilities, I'd calibrate them first.
total cost(t) = FP(t) × ₹100 + FN(t) × ₹5,000     →  choose t with the lowest cost on validation data
  • Raising the threshold flags fewer cases: precision usually rises, recall falls.
  • Calibration (Platt scaling or isotonic regression, CalibratedClassifierCV) makes a score of 0.3 really mean "30% chance".

5. Your model was great offline but is worse in production. Why?

Say it in an interviewI'd check four things. Training-serving skew: features computed differently in the live system than in my notebook. Data drift: live inputs look different, for example new kinds of users or a new season. Concept drift: the relationship itself changed, like fraudsters adapting. And leakage that made the offline score fake in the first place. To find out, I'd log the live features and compare their distributions with training, replay live data through the offline pipeline, and break the errors down by segment and time.

6. How do you handle missing values and outliers?

Say it in an interviewFirst I ask why values are missing, because missingness itself can carry information, for example high earners skipping the income field. Then: drop a column that's mostly empty, impute the median for skewed numbers and "Unknown" for categories, add an is_missing flag, and fit the imputer on training data only. Tree boosters like XGBoost can handle missing values directly. For outliers, I check whether they're errors; if they're real, I cap them, log-transform, or use a robust model, and in fraud the outliers may be exactly what we're looking for.

Missing values

  • Why missing? Completely at random (safe to impute), depends on other columns (impute with a model), or depends on the value itself (add a flag, since the missingness is informative).
  • Options: drop rows (only if few and random); drop the column (mostly missing); median, mode or "Unknown"; KNN or iterative imputation; is_missing indicator; models that handle NaN.

Outliers

  • Detect: the IQR rule (below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR), z-score above 3, or domain rules (age 250 is an error).
  • Treat: fix or drop errors; for real extremes, cap at the 1st and 99th percentiles (winsorise), log-transform skewed values, or use robust models (trees, Huber loss).

7. How would you explain feature importance to a non-technical stakeholder, and what are its limits?

Say it in an interviewFor the whole model, I'd use permutation importance: "if we scramble 'days since last login', the model's accuracy drops the most, so that's what it relies on most." For one customer, SHAP values: "the average churn risk is 20%; this customer is at 72%, and 30 points of that come from not logging in for 30 days." The limits: importance shows what the model uses, not what causes the outcome; correlated features share or swap importance; and a suspiciously important feature can be a sign of leakage.
  • Built-in tree importance (impurity-based) is quick but favours features with many unique values.
  • Permutation importance: shuffle one feature on validation data and measure how much the score drops.
  • SHAP: splits each prediction into contributions per feature that add up to (prediction − average prediction).
  • Never say "increase X and churn will fall" from importance alone; that needs an experiment.

Product sense

Success-metric questions: "How would you measure the success of Instagram Reels?"

The framework (say it in this order)1. Clarify the goal of the product. 2. Who are the users and what do they do? 3. One primary (north-star) metric. 4. Two or three supporting metrics. 5. Guardrails: what must not get worse. 6. How you'd measure it (A/B test, time window).

Worked answer

  1. Goal: keep users engaged with short video and grow creators (competing with YouTube Shorts).
  2. Users: viewers (watch, like, share) and creators (post reels).
  3. Primary: share of daily active users who watch at least one reel, or reel watch time per daily active user.
  4. Supporting: reels shared per viewer (quality signal), creators posting at least one reel a week, 7-day retention of people who watch reels.
  5. Guardrails: time on feed and stories (are reels just stealing from them?), total app time, hide or report rate, ad revenue per user.
  6. Measure: A/B test changes against these, and watch week-over-week trends for the launch itself.

Practise with: Swiggy Instamart, YouTube Shorts, UPI payments in an app, LinkedIn "Easy Apply", a new Spotify playlist feature. Use the same six steps.

Metric-drop questions: "Swiggy orders fell 8% last week. How do you investigate?"

The framework (say it in this order)1. Clarify the metric and the comparison. 2. Rule out data problems. 3. Check internal changes and outside events. 4. Segment to see where the drop sits. 5. Break the metric into a funnel to see which step dropped. 6. Form a hypothesis, confirm it, suggest a fix.

Worked answer

  1. Clarify: orders placed or delivered? Compared with the week before, or the same week last year? Sudden or gradual?
  2. Data problems: a logging change, a broken dashboard, a pipeline delay. Do other sources (payments, restaurant records) also show the drop?
  3. Changes and events: an app release, a price or delivery-fee change, an outage; outside, a festival, a cricket final, heavy rain, or a competitor's offer.
  4. Segment: Android vs iOS and app version, city, new vs returning users, cuisine, time of day. A drop in only one segment points to the cause.
  5. Funnel: find which step's rate fell (the diagram above).
  6. Conclude: "Payment success fell on Android version 5.2 after Tuesday's release, so I'd roll back the payment SDK change and confirm orders recover."
